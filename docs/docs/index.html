0. Abstract

This study explores whether historical search-performance signals can help prioritize content pages for human review. An anonymized dataset containing 30,000 records and 44 columns was examined using a transparent baseline ranking and a logistic-regression model. The exploratory target was derived from an existing trend-direction field, and evaluation used a client-grouped train/test split to reduce client overlap between the two sets. The model achieved average precision of 0.5790 and precision@20 of 0.85 on the held-out split, compared with 0.5124 and 0.35 for the baseline. These results show a difference in ranking performance against the available proxy label, not proof of future decline or evidence that updating a page will improve its performance.

Decision: Use the ranking as a starting point for human review—not as an automatic content-update or removal system.

1. Introduction and Problem

Content teams may have more pages to review than they can investigate at once. A useful prioritization method should help reviewers decide where to look first while remaining understandable and reproducible.

This project asks whether historical search signals can help order pages for review. It compares a simple scoring baseline with a machine-learning approach and documents the limitations of using a trend-derived proxy target.

Objective

  • Build a transparent baseline for ranking review candidates.
  • Compare the baseline with logistic regression using a client-grouped holdout.
  • Translate the results into cautious, human-reviewed recommendations.

3. Methodology

Baseline: ML-07

A transparent additive score used available historical signals such as 90-day impressions, click-through rate, content age, time since last update, and word-count availability. The ranking was ordered by score, then impressions, then content identifier as a deterministic tie-breaker.

Model: ML-08

Logistic regression was evaluated against the proxy target. A client-grouped train/test split used random state 42. The training set contained 23,837 records across 25 clients; the test set contained 6,163 records across 7 different clients, with no client overlap.

Evaluation

Average precision (AP) and ROC-AUC summarize ranking/discrimination behavior. Precision@20 measures the share of the top 20 ranked records matching the proxy label. Metrics describe this particular split and label definition only.

Important: A client-grouped split reduces one form of leakage, but does not establish prospective forecasting performance or causal impact.

5. Limitations

  • Proxy label: The target reflects an existing trend-direction field, not an independently measured future outcome.
  • No causal conclusion: The analysis cannot show that refreshing a page causes search performance to improve.
  • Limited generalization: The held-out split included keyword articles only.
  • Missing data: Missing word counts and other availability patterns may influence scores and model behavior.
  • Potential confounding: Associations may reflect content type, measurement coverage, or other factors rather than an actionable cause.
  • Exploratory evaluation: These recorded metrics do not replace a prospective time-based evaluation or a sealed final test.
  • Human review required: A high score is a review signal, not a verdict that a page is poor or should be changed.

6. Ranked Recommendations

  1. Review high-priority pages manually. Check intent match, content accuracy, usefulness, and search-result context before taking action.
  2. Audit missing measurements. Investigate missing word counts and zero-position values before interpreting them as content problems.
  3. Use CTR as a diagnostic signal, not a standalone rule. Review impressions, query intent, and result appearance together.
  4. Consider age and update recency in context. Old content is not automatically outdated; verify whether facts, links, or guidance need attention.
  5. Validate across content types. Test on suitable held-out examples from comparison and other content categories before broader use.
  6. Run a prospective evaluation. Freeze the scoring method, evaluate on a genuinely later period, and track outcomes before claiming forecasting value.
  7. Keep people in control. Record reviewer decisions and reasons, then use them to improve future evaluation responsibly.
